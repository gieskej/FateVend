# Genre - Nihongi

> From Nihon Shoki, give me  a summary of monsters and deities
The *Nihon Shoki* (Chronicles of Japan, compiled in 720 CE) is one of Japan's oldest and most vital historical and mythological texts. Unlike later medieval folklore which is full of playful, localized *yōkai* (like *kappa* or *tanuki*), the supernatural entities in the *Nihon Shoki* are larger-than-life cosmic forces, primordial deities (*kami*), and terrifying, untamed beasts that the early imperial lineage had to subdue.

Here is a summary of the prominent deities and monsters featured in the chronicle.

---

## The Deities (Kami)

The text divides deities into two main categories: the **Amatsukami** (Heavenly Gods) who dwell in the High Celestial Plain (*Takamagahara*), and the **Kunitsukami** (Earthly Gods) who inhabit the physical world.

### 1. The Primordial Creators

* **Kotoamatsukami & Invisible Deities:** The chronicle begins with the spontaneous manifestation of solitary gods when heaven and earth split. The prime figure often highlighted is **Ame-no-Minakanushi** (The Heavenly Center Lord), representing the cosmic source.
* **Izanagi and Izanami:** The divine couple tasked with solidifying the drifting earth. Stirring the brine with a jeweled spear, they created the first islands. They gave birth to the topography of Japan and a host of nature deities before Izanami died from burning while giving birth to the fire god, Kagutsuchi.

### 2. The Three Noble Children

Born from Izanagi’s ritual purification after he fled the underworld (*Yomi-no-kuni*):

* **Amaterasu Ōmikami (The Sun Goddess):** Given rule over the heavens. She is the central figure of the *Nihon Shoki*, portrayed as both a radiant ruler and the direct ancestor of the Japanese Imperial line.
* **Tsukuyomi-no-Mikoto (The Moon God):** Allocated control over the night. In the *Nihon Shoki*, he famously kills the food goddess Ukemochi out of disgust, causing Amaterasu to separate from him forever (explaining the division of day and night).
* **Susanoo-no-Mikoto (The Storm God):** A wild, chaotic deity given dominion over the seas. His destructive tantrums in heaven drive Amaterasu into a cave, plunging the world into darkness, which forces the other gods to trick her out using a mirror and dance. He is later banished to earth, transitioning into a heroic protector figure.

### 3. Martial and Pacifying Deities

* **Takemikazuchi & Futsunushi:** Swordsmen gods sent from heaven to conquer the unruly earthly deities and prepare Japan for the descent of Amaterasu’s grandson, Ninigi.
* **Ōkuninushi:** The primary ruler of the earthly realm. He is portrayed as a benevolent but powerful god who builds the world but ultimately relinquishes political control to the heavenly lineage in exchange for dominion over the unseen spiritual world.

---

## The Monsters and Untamed Spirits

Monsters in the *Nihon Shoki* are rarely just "evil beasts"; they are usually manifestations of raw, chaotic nature or defiant indigenous forces resisting imperial order.

### 1. Yamata no Orochi (The Eight-Headed Serpent)

Perhaps the most famous monster in the text. It is described as a colossal serpent spanning eight valleys and eight hills, with eyes as red as winter cherries, cypress and fir trees growing on its back, and a belly perpetually bloody with inflammation. It terrorized the land of Izumo, devouring daughters until **Susanoo** defeated it by getting all eight heads drunk on eightfold-refined sake and hacking it to pieces. Inside its tail, he discovered the legendary *Kusanagi-no-Tsurugi* (the grass-cutting sword).

### 2. The Thunder Gods of Yomi (Ikazuchi-no-Kami)

When Izanagi traveled to the underworld to rescue Izanami, he broke a taboo by looking at her corpse. He discovered her flesh rotting and hosting **eight fierce Thunder Gods** (*Ō-ikazuchi*, *Hono-ikazuchi*, etc.). These monstrous, howling configurations of lightning and decay chased Izanagi to the borders of the living world until he warded them off by throwing peaches at them.

### 3. Tsuchigumo (The Earth Spiders)

While later folklore turned *Tsuchigumo* into giant, literal spider-monsters with supernatural illusions, the *Nihon Shoki* uses the term fluidly. It refers to a race of short-limbed, cave-dwelling indigenous peoples who defied the expansion of the Yamato court, described as having "the hearts of wolves and the manners of spiders." However, they are frequently written with monstrous, uncanny characteristics, blurring the line between human rebels and subterranean beasts.

### 4. Yato-no-Kami (The Horned Serpents)

Though heavily featured in the related *Hitachi Fudoki* (regional records from the same era), these are the slithering snake-deities with horns on their heads. Seeing one was said to wipe out an entire family line. They represented the untamed marshlands that aggressively resisted human agricultural cultivation.

---

## Key Cultural Theme

The interplay between deities and monsters in the *Nihon Shoki* serves a distinct purpose: **the transition from chaos to order**. The text systematically illustrates how wild, chaotic monsters or vengeful nature spirits are either pacified, slain, or transformed into benevolent protector deities through ritual, cunning, and imperial authority.



> Give summary of later folklore
As Japan transitioned from the classical era into the medieval and early modern periods (spanning the Muromachi to the Edo periods), the supernatural underwent a massive cultural shift. The terrifying, cosmic forces and defiant indigenous spirits of the *Nihon Shoki* were gradually replaced by **yōkai**—a highly diverse class of monsters, spirits, and ghosts that were deeply localized, often playful, and inextricably tied to daily human life.

Instead of threatening the imperial court, these spirits existed to explain the unexplainable bumps in the night, teach moral lessons, or simply entertain.

---

## The Shift: From Divine to Domestic

In medieval folklore, many monsters were born from the concept of *Tsukumogami*—the belief that ordinary household objects (like umbrellas, sandals, or sake jars) could acquire a soul and come to life after serving for 100 years. This shifted the supernatural from grand wildernesses straight into the human home, giving rise to creatures that were mischievous rather than murderous.

---

## The Iconic Medieval & Later Yōkai

### 1. The Animal Shape-Shifters (Henge)

In later folklore, certain animals were believed to possess spiritual energy capable of tricking humans.

* **Tanuki (Bake-danuki):** The Japanese raccoon dog transformed from a sinister, soul-eating creature in early myths into a jolly, beer-loving prankster. They use magic leaves to shape-shift, trick human merchants with illusionary money, and are famous for their giant, magical scrotums which they stretch out to use as drums, boats, or cloaks.

* **Kitsune (The Fox):** While some foxes remained sacred messengers of the rice god Inari, wild foxes (*yako*) became legendary tricksters. They frequently shape-shifted into beautiful women to marry unsuspecting men or trick arrogant samurai, though they were easily exposed if they drank too much and let their tails show.

### 2. The Water Trickster: Kappa

The **Kappa** is perhaps the most famous localized yōkai. A child-sized, amphibious creature with webbed hands, a turtle-like shell, and a duck-like beak, the Kappa dwells in rivers and ponds.

* **The Vulnerability:** It derives its immense strength from a shallow cavity (*sara*) on top of its head filled with water. If a human bows deeply to a Kappa, its polite nature forces it to bow back, spilling the water and rendering it completely helpless.

* **The Behavior:** While they were occasionally blamed for drowning livestock, they were more famous for stealing cucumbers (their favorite food) and trying to extract a mythical organ called the *shirikodama* from human rectums.

### 3. The Urban & Domestic Spooks

* **Karakasa-kozō (The Paper Umbrella Ghost):** A classic *tsukumogami* depicted as a folded paper umbrella with a single large eye, a long tongue, and a single leg wearing a wooden clog (*geta*). It hops around playfully, startling humans but rarely causing real harm.

* **Rokurokubi:** Elusive human-looking yōkai (usually women) whose necks stretch to incredible lengths at night while their bodies sleep. They wander the house to lick lamp oil or scare people, often completely unaware of their own monstrous nature during the day.

* **Azukiarai (The Bean Washer):** A harmless, localized spirit found near rivers. Humans would hear the distinct sound of someone washing red azuki beans, accompanied by a singing voice. If you got too close, it would simply splash into the water and disappear.

---

## Night Parade of One Hundred Demons (*Hyakki Yagyō*)

By the Muromachi period, this folklore culminated in the popular artistic concept of the **Hyakki Yagyō** (The Night Parade of One Hundred Demons). Instead of a terrifying apocalypse, scroll paintings depicted a chaotic, almost comedic midnight parade where hundreds of different yōkai—from standard demons to animated kitchen utensils—marched through the streets of Kyoto. If a human looked upon the parade, they would die, unless they carried a protective Buddhist amulet.

---

## Summary of the Evolution

| Feature | Ancient Mythology (*Nihon Shoki*) | Later Medieval/Edo Folklore |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Scale** | Cosmic, nationwide, epic | Localized, neighborhood-specific, domestic |
| **Nature** | Chaotic forces to be conquered or killed | Mischievous tricksters to be outsmarted or coexisted with |
| **Origin** | Divine bloodlines, decay, nature raw | Aged household objects, shape-shifting animals, human quirks |
| **Tone** | Grim, political, religious | Playful, satirical, entertaining |


> Aren't there also folklore about vengeful female ghosts?  Where did that come from?

Yes, absolutely. In fact, vengeful female ghosts—known primarily as **Onryō** (vengeful spirits) or **Yūrei** (general ghosts)—form some of the most terrifying, enduring, and psychologically complex corners of Japanese folklore.

While *yōkai* like the Kappa or Tanuki are often tied to nature or mischief, these female ghosts are intensely human. Their stories almost always stem from betrayal, tragic systemic oppression, and a wrath so fierce that it tears through the boundary of death.

Here is a breakdown of where this folklore came from and how it evolved.

---

## The Origins: Where Did They Come From?

The archetype of the vengeful female spirit didn’t appear overnight; it evolved through a mix of early religious fears, societal structures, and theatrical traditions.

### 1. Shinto and the Concept of *Kegare* (Pollution)

In Japan’s indigenous Shinto belief system, a violent or sudden death disrupts cosmic harmony, creating spiritual pollution (*kegare*). If a person dies carrying a massive burden of rage, jealousy, or despair, their soul (*reikon*) cannot move on to the afterlife. Instead, it transforms into an *Onryō*—a spirit of pure vengeance that haunts the living world until its wrath is pacified.

### 2. The Oppression of Women in Feudal Society

During the Heian, Kamakura, and Edo periods, women in Japan’s strictly patriarchal society often had very little social, legal, or political power. They were largely dependent on their husbands or families.

* **Power in Death:** If a woman was abused, discarded for a younger concubine, or falsely accused, she had no recourse in life. Folklore became a psychological mirror: **death was the only equalizer**. An oppressed woman could do nothing in the physical world, but as an *Onryō*, her wrath was absolute, capable of striking down entire lineages, causing plagues, or driving her abusers mad.

### 3. The Influence of Buddhist Karma and Theatre

In the medieval period, the rise of **Noh** and **Kabuki** theater took localized ghost stories and popularized them across the country.

* **The Hannya Mask:** In Noh theater, the famous *Hannya* mask (a grinning, horned demonic face) specifically represents a woman whose intense jealousy and grief have transformed her into a living demon.
* These plays hammered home a Buddhist moral lesson: extreme negative emotions bind you to the mortal coil and distort your humanity.

---

## Iconic Examples of Vengeful Female Ghosts

### Oiwa (*Yotsuya Kaidan*)

Oiwa is arguably the most famous *Yūrei* in Japanese history. Her story, popularized in the Edo period, follows a loyal wife whose samurai husband, Iemon, wants to marry a wealthy neighbor's granddaughter. Iemon poisons Oiwa, which doesn't kill her immediately but horribly disfigures her face, causing her eye to droop and her hair to fall out in bloody clumps. Upon realizing the betrayal, Oiwa dies in despair and returns to ruthlessly haunt Iemon, tricking him into decapitating his new bride and father-in-law by making them look like her ghostly face.

### Okiku (*Banchō Sarayashiki*)

Okiku was a servant girl who rejected the romantic advances of a samurai named Aoyama. Enraged, Aoyama hid one of ten precious family plates entrusted to her care and threatened to frame her for theft unless she complied. Instead, Okiku drowned herself in a well. Her ghost would rise from the well every night, slowly and dismally counting the plates: *"One... two... three... up to nine."* Upon reaching nine, she would let out a bloodcurdling, agonizing shriek that drove anyone who heard it insane.

### Kuchisake-Onna (The Slit-Mouthed Woman)

Though it morphed into a massive "urban legend" panic in the late 1970s, this legend has roots in Edo-period folklore. It tells of a beautiful woman mutilated by a jealous husband who slit her mouth from ear to ear. As a ghost, she approaches mortals wearing a mask and asks, *"Am I beautiful?"* If they say yes, she removes the mask to reveal her scars and asks again. A "yes" results in her slicing the victim's mouth to match hers; a "no" results in instant death.

## The Signature Look: Creating the Modern Horror Aesthetic

If you've ever seen modern Japanese horror films like *The Ring* (Sadako) or *The Grudge* (Kayako), you are looking directly at traditional Edo-period folklore. The classic look of the female ghost was heavily standardized by Kabuki theater:

* **The White Kimono (*Katabira*):** Represents the burial shroud.
* **Long, Unbound Black Hair:** In feudal Japan, women kept their hair neatly tied up. Wild, loose hair was a visual shorthand for madness, chaos, and a lack of social restraint.
* **Lack of Feet:** In woodblock prints (*ukiyo-e*), female ghosts are often depicted floating, their bodies tapering off into mist with no feet, emphasizing their detachment from the physical earth.
* **The Ghostly Hands:** Limp, dangling wrists held close to the chest, a classic theatrical pose signifying a lack of life force.
